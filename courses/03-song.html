Part 0

Why this session exists

松 sōng is the principle every teacher names first and beginners understand last, and the reason is a translation accident. The usual English word is "relax," and "relax" imports collapse - the couch, the sigh, the shoulders folding forward. That is the opposite of what is meant. Today: what 松 actually is, the ten body requirements Yang Chengfu dictated to a student who published them in 1925, and why one phrase everyone quotes as ancient wisdom has a date on it that most people would be surprised by.

Part 1 · covers the central principle

松 sōng: released, not collapsed 7 min live

松 sōng, or 放松 fàngsōng in its everyday two-character form, means releasing unnecessary holding in the joints and muscles while keeping structure. Both halves matter. Drop the holding and keep the structure and you have 松. Drop both and you have a heap.

LiveWhat 松 actually asks for3 min▶

Notice what the diagram does to the usual mental model. Most beginners arrive holding a single dial in their head, running from "tense" to "relaxed," and they assume the instruction is to turn the dial down. That model has only two states and it cannot express 松 at all, because on that dial the far end is collapse.

The real picture has three states, and the useful question is not "how relaxed am I" but "what is holding me up right now?" If the answer is gripped muscle, you are stiff. If the answer is nothing, you are collapsed. If the answer is your skeleton, stacked and aligned, with the muscles doing only the work that is left over, you are somewhere near 松.

The teaching image the tradition uses is "iron wrapped in cotton" - something soft on the outside with something unyielding running through it. And 松 is almost never taught alone: it is paired with 掤 péng, the buoyant outward supportive quality that keeps the released body from going flat. Session 4 gives 掤 its proper treatment as one of the eight gates. For now, hold them as a pair, because 松 without 掤 is just collapse with better vocabulary.

Real world

Why your first class may confuse you. A teacher will tell you to relax your shoulders, and then thirty seconds later tell you that your arms have gone dead and to lift them. Both instructions are correct and they are not in conflict - the first is about releasing the holding, the second is about keeping the structure. If you know the three states exist, that exchange makes sense. If you do not, it feels like the teacher is contradicting themselves.

LiveThe classical anchor: soft first, then hard2.5 min▶

The text says. The line that settles the argument comes from 十三勢行工心解, one of the canonical texts in the 19th-century manuscript tradition:

✦ 十三勢行工心解极柔软，然后极坚刚。 Jí róuruǎn, ránhòu jí jiāngāng. "Extremely soft, and only then extremely hard - firm."

Read the grammar rather than the poetry. 然后 ránhòu means "and then," "after that." It sets up a sequence, not a contrast. The text is not saying "be soft instead of hard," and it is not saying "be soft and hard at the same time" - it is saying softness is what firmness is built out of, and it comes first in the order of operations.

That single grammatical point is the whole reason 松 is not laziness. You are not releasing in order to be comfortable. You are releasing because the thing you eventually want cannot be assembled any other way.

Yang Chengfu states the same idea from the other direction, in the fifth of his Ten Essentials, by describing what stiffness costs you:

✦ Yang Chengfu, Essential 5 · 沉肩坠肘"If you lift your shoulders, the qi will rise with them and the whole body will be without strength."

Note the shape of that claim. It is not "lifted shoulders look bad." It is that holding costs you power - the stiffness does not add force, it subtracts it. That is the same argument as 极柔软，然后极坚刚, made in the vocabulary of the body rather than the vocabulary of sequence.

Self-studyWhy it is hard, and why that claim is not research2 min read▶

You will read, in a great many places including this page, that 松 is the hardest thing for beginners. Here is the honest label: that is practitioner consensus, not research. No institutional source, no trial, no measurement. It is what experienced teachers say, repeated often enough that it sounds established. It may well be true. It is not evidenced, and this course will not dress it up as if it were.

What can be said more carefully is structural. Adults arrive at a first class with decades of habitual holding they did not choose and cannot feel, because habitual muscular tension stops being noticeable roughly the moment it becomes habitual. So the instruction "release your shoulders" asks you to let go of something you do not currently know you are doing. That is a genuinely awkward instruction, and it explains the difficulty without needing to claim a finding that does not exist.

Part 2 · covers the body method

The Ten Essentials 太极拳十要, in the right order 8 min live

If 松 is the principle, the Ten Essentials are the checklist. They are the single most quoted body-method document in tai chi, they turn up on the wall of a great many training halls, and casual English lists of them frequently get the order wrong. Here they are with their attribution stated properly and their sequence verified.

LiveWho said it, who wrote it, and when3 min▶

The Ten Essentials were orally transmitted by 杨澄甫 Yang Chengfu (1883-1936), the grandson of Yang Luchan and the man who standardised the Yang form most of the world now practises. He did not write them down himself. They were recorded by his student 陈微明 Chen Weiming and first published in 1925, in Chen Weiming's book 《太极拳术》. They appear again later in Yang Chengfu's own 《太极拳体用全书》 of 1934.

The hedge worth carrying. Chen Weiming positions himself in the text as a recorder - the student taking down what the master said. That framing is conventional and it is also convenient. He was a literate man writing up spoken lectures, and he very likely reworked them. So the safest description is not "Yang Chengfu wrote" and not "Chen Weiming invented," but a 1925 published text, attributed to Yang Chengfu's teaching, in Chen Weiming's prose.

Why the date matters more than it looks 1925 is not ancient. It is later than the telephone. The Wu and Li family manuscripts that carry the classical canon date to the 1860s-1880s, and the events those texts describe are older still. The Ten Essentials sit on top of that literature as a modern teaching summary of it - excellent, influential, and a completely different textual layer. Part 3 shows what happens when people forget that.
LiveThe ten, in the verified order4 min▶

Read down the list once before reading the notes. The sequence itself is informative: it starts at the crown of the head and works down the body, and then, halfway through, it stops being about anatomy at all.

#汉字PinyinPlain English
1虚灵顶劲xū líng dǐng jìnempty, lively energy at the crown; head suspended, neck not stiff
2含胸拔背hán xiōng bá bèicontain the chest, pluck up the back (this is what enables 气沉丹田)
3松腰sōng yāoloosen the waist - "the commander of the whole body"
4分虚实fēn xū shídistinguish empty and full - Yang Chengfu's "number one rule"
5沉肩坠肘chén jiān zhuì zhǒusink the shoulders, drop the elbows
6用意不用力yòng yì bù yòng lìuse intent, not brute force
7上下相随shàng xià xiāng suíupper and lower body follow each other
8内外相合nèi wài xiāng héinner and outer unite
9相连不断xiāng lián bù duànlinked together without interruption
10动中求静dòng zhōng qiú jìngseek stillness within movement

The common structural reading is 1-5 body mechanics, 6-10 more abstract. Worth labelling honestly: that split is a reader's convention rather than a heading in the text. But it is a good one, and it explains why the first half is teachable from a diagram and the second half is not.

LiveTwo you have already met2 min▶
  • No. 4, 分虚实 fēn xū shí, is what Session 2's lab measured. Yang Chengfu calls it the number one rule and states it without any numbers: shift onto one leg, that leg is 实 shí solid and the other is 虚 xū empty, and only then are turns "light, nimble and almost without effort." The lab gave you the geometry behind that sentence - and reminded you that the 70/30 and 100/0 percentages are modern teaching convention, absent from the classics.
  • No. 3, 松腰 sōng yāo, is 松 applied to one specific place - and it is the place the text singles out, calling the waist "the commander of the whole body." Set that beside the rooting line from Session 2, 其根在脚，发于腿，主宰于腰，形于手指, "the root is in the feet, issued through the legs, governed by the waist, expressed in the fingers." Same claim, two documents: the waist is the hinge everything upstream and downstream passes through, so a gripped waist breaks the chain in the middle.

This is the most useful habit you can build with the Essentials: do not treat them as ten separate rules. Numbers 1, 2, 3 and 5 are all the same instruction - release this joint without losing this structure - applied at the crown, the torso, the waist and the shoulders in turn.

Self-studyWhere to read them in full1 min read▶

Two free, reliable English versions exist. The Yang Family official site carries a translation by Jerry Karin, and Brennan Translation carries a bilingual version with the Chinese alongside. Read either with the dating in mind: you are reading a 1925 lecture summary, not a classical scripture, and it is a better document for being read that way.

Part 3 · covers the most misdated phrase in tai chi

用意不用力 - dated correctly 7 min live

"Use intent, not force." It is on posters. It is the epigraph of a hundred articles. It gets introduced as timeless Daoist wisdom from the deep past. It is Yang Chengfu's sixth essential, and it was published in 1925.

LiveWhere it actually comes from3 min▶

Put the two textual layers side by side and the gap is obvious once you look at it:

  • The Wu and Li family classics - 太极拳论, 十三势行工歌诀, 打手歌, and the rest - are known to us from manuscripts written inside one family circle in 永年 Yongnian in the 1860s to 1880s. Li Yiyu's handwritten manual for Hao He is dated 1881.
  • 用意不用力 is Yang Chengfu's Essential 6, published 1925. That is roughly 45 to 60 years later, in a different genre, by a different hand, for a different audience.

So when a school website prints 用意不用力 under a heading like "from the tai chi classics," the claim is simply false - not maliciously, just carelessly. The phrase belongs to the modern pedagogical layer, alongside the rest of the Ten Essentials. It is a very good instruction. It is not a classical one.

Why this course keeps doing this Dating is not pedantry here, it is the whole method. A tradition that cannot tell its 1925 material from its 1881 material also cannot tell you which of its claims have been tested by four hundred years of use and which are one influential teacher's good idea from the Republican era. Both are worth having. Knowing which is which is what lets you evaluate the next confident claim you hear.
LiveWhat Yang Chengfu's own text actually claims2.5 min▶

Here is the part that surprises people who only know the slogan. Read in full, Essential 6 is not mystical at all. It is a concrete physiological argument in the vocabulary of traditional Chinese medicine:

✦ Yang Chengfu, Essential 6 · 用意不用力 (1925)用意不用力 yòng yì bù yòng lì "Don't employ even the tiniest amount of coarse strength, which would cause musculoskeletal or circulatory blockage." "If you move the body about with stiff force, you swamp the meridians. Chi and blood are impeded."

That is a claim about plumbing. Stiffness obstructs; obstruction impedes flow; impeded flow costs you function. Whether you accept 经络 jīngluò meridians as a description of anatomy is a separate question, and Session 5 handles it squarely. What matters here is the shape of the argument: Yang Chengfu is not saying "think your movements into existence." He is saying that gripping is mechanically and physiologically expensive, which is the same thing Essential 5 said about lifted shoulders.

LiveThe classical ancestor, and why it is not the source2 min▶

There is a genuinely older idea in this neighbourhood. It is a different phrase, from 十三势行工歌诀:

✦ 十三势行工歌诀意气君来骨肉臣。 Yì qì jūn lái gǔ ròu chén. "Intent and qi are sovereign; bone and flesh are subject."

Same family of thought: attention leads, the meat follows. But notice the relationship carefully. This is a paraphrase relationship, not a source relationship. The characters are different, the metaphor is different - the older line uses an imperial court image of ruler and minister, which 用意不用力 does not - and nothing in the record shows Yang Chengfu deriving one from the other. What you can honestly say is that a 1925 instruction has a conceptual ancestor in the 19th-century canon. What you cannot honestly say is that 用意不用力 comes from the classics.

Self-studyWhat it means in practice is interpretive2 min read▶

The hedge, stated plainly: what 用意不用力 means in practice is interpretive, and modern schools mystify it in both directions.

  • Inflation. Some treat 意 yì as a literal projected energy - something that leaves the body and acts at a distance, with the empty-force demonstrations that come with that reading. Nothing in Yang Chengfu's text supports it.
  • Deflation. Others flatten the whole essential into "just relax," which loses the word 意 entirely and collapses the phrase back into the translation error Part 1 was about.

Yang Chengfu's own text sits between the two, as a claim about stiffness impeding circulation. And here is a plain-English gloss you can carry into a class without committing to any metaphysics:

An honest working gloss 用意不用力 is an instruction about effort allocation and attention. Two halves. First: do not add muscular effort where structure and timing would do the work. Second: lead the movement with attention rather than with muscular push - decide where you are going before you shove yourself there. Neither half requires you to believe anything you do not believe, and both are things a teacher can actually correct you on.
Reflect & apply · the song audit

Find out where you hold ✦ 10 min

You cannot release holding you cannot feel, so the first job is not releasing, it is noticing. This is a body scan for grip, done standing, working from the top down. Nothing here should hurt and nothing here is an exercise - you are gathering information.

Stand comfortably and work down the list. Jaw, neck, shoulders, hands, chest, belly, hips, knees, toes. At each place ask one question: "am I gripping here for no reason?" Do not fix anything yet. Just find out. Most people are startled by the jaw and by the toes.

Release, and then check what happened. At each place where the answer was yes, let it go - and immediately apply the test: did you get shorter, or did you just get softer? Shorter means you collapsed. Softer at the same height means 松. This one question is the whole session in a portable form.

Note your top three. Write down the three places you hold most. They will be reliable, they will follow you around, and they are what you will be working on for months. Knowing them before your first class means you can tell a teacher something useful when they ask.

The structure test, no partner needed. Let your arms float in front of you, roughly chest height, elbows a little lower than the wrists. Now soften the shoulders without letting the arms drop. That floating-but-not-lifted feeling - held, but not by effort at the shoulder - is 掤 péng and 松 sōng working together. It is also the first thing that will disappear the moment you get tired, which is useful to know about yourself.

If the audit finds pain rather than tension Holding and pain are different findings and the difference matters. Tension releases when you notice it; pain does not. If a place hurts rather than grips, leave it alone, write it down, and put it on the list of things to mention to a prospective teacher in Session 6. "Which movements bother my shoulder" is one of the most useful sentences a beginner can say out loud.

Your posture checklist ✦ 8 min · builds your kit

Step 3 of the readiness kit turns the Ten Essentials from a wall poster into something you own. The instruction is deliberately awkward: write the five mechanical essentials in your own plain words, not the translations above. If you cannot phrase one, that is the one you have not understood yet, and that is a useful thing to discover on paper rather than in a room full of people.

Rewrite five essentials in your own words. Numbers 1, 2, 3 and 5 from today, plus number 4 from Session 2. One short line each. No borrowed phrasing.

Add your top three holding places from the audit above, with the date, so you can check them again in three months.

Write the one-line definition at the bottom and leave it there. It is the sentence that will keep you out of trouble in your first month.

Homework

Before Session 4 ◐ 2 min a day

  • The daily two-minute song audit. Same scan, top to bottom, once a day. Two minutes, standing, anywhere. The point is not to release more each time - it is to shorten the delay between holding and noticing, which is the actual trainable skill.
  • Catch yourself holding in ordinary life. Screen time, driving, queueing, waiting for a kettle. Jaw and shoulders are the usual suspects, and both are free to check at any moment without anyone noticing. When you find one, apply the test: release, then check whether you got shorter or just softer.
  • Watch the 24-form once more, and this time watch a practitioner's shoulders rather than their hands. A good one keeps them where they are while the arms travel. That is Essential 5 in visible form.
  • Optional: read the Ten Essentials in full, either at the Yang Family official site (Jerry Karin's translation) or at Brennan Translation for the Chinese alongside. Ten short paragraphs; twenty minutes at most.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · 松 sōng means...

Three states, not two. A is collapse, C is stiffness, and 松 is the middle one. The test after any release is whether you got shorter (collapse) or just softer (song). 极柔软，然后极坚刚 - softness is the precondition of firmness, not its absence.

2 · The Ten Essentials 太极拳十要 reached print...

Orally transmitted by Yang Chengfu (1883-1936), written down by his student 陈微明 Chen Weiming, first published 1925 in 《太极拳术》. They appear again in Yang's own 1934 book, but 1925 is the first publication - and Chen Weiming very likely reworked the lectures he says he only recorded.

3 · 用意不用力 "use intent, not force" comes from...

Roughly 45 to 60 years later than the Wu and Li manuscripts, and a different textual layer entirely. The classics do contain a conceptual ancestor, 意气君来骨肉臣, but that is a paraphrase relationship rather than a source.
