Part 0

Why this session exists

Tai chi arrives with more myth attached than almost any other physical practice. The good news is that the myth is not the interesting part. The documented history is better: a 17th-century village art, carried to Beijing by a man who learned it in 1820, rewritten for public health by a committee in 1956, and now practised in parks on every continent. This session gives you that story, the honest version, so nothing in the next five sessions has to rest on a legend.

Part 1 · covers what the practice is

Three things at once 6 min live

Ask three practitioners what tai chi is and you can get three different answers, all correct. It is a martial art. It is a health practice. It is a moving meditation. These are not marketing angles layered onto one thing - they are three genuine identities the practice has held, sometimes uneasily, and the tension between them is historically real.

LiveWhat each identity actually claims3 min▶
  • Martial art. This is the oldest identity and the most neglected. Every posture in a form was a fighting technique before it was a health movement. Most modern classes never train the applications, which is fine - but it explains why the movements are shaped the way they are, and why they look strange if you assume they are calisthenics.
  • Health practice. NCCIH, the US National Center for Complementary and Integrative Health, puts it plainly: "Tai chi originated as an ancient martial art in China. Over the years, it has become more focused on health promotion and rehabilitation." That shift was deliberate and datable - see the self-study card below.
  • Moving meditation. Harvard Health uses the phrase "meditation in motion," and adds the pun that "it might well be called 'medication in motion'." This is the identity most practitioners report as the reason they stay.

UNESCO's official description - the one attached to its 2020 heritage listing - manages to hold all three: a "traditional physical practice characterized by relaxed, circular movements that works in concert with breath regulation and the cultivation of a righteous and neutral mind," built on five steps, eight techniques, routines and partner work, "influenced by Daoist and Confucian thought and theories of traditional Chinese medicine."

Real world

Why this matters for choosing a class. A teacher whose centre of gravity is the martial identity will drill stance and application and may find your questions about stress relief beside the point. A teacher rooted in the health identity may never mention that the posture you are learning is a throw. Neither is wrong. But if you walk in without knowing the three identities exist, you can end up in the wrong room and conclude tai chi is not for you.

Self-studyWhen the health identity took over2 min read▶

The pivot is not ancient. In early 20th-century China, martial arts were consciously reframed as fitness-as-nation-building, and teachers were commissioned to teach for health and strengthening rather than for fighting. Two institutions did most of the work: the Jingwu Association (from around 1910) and the Central Guoshu Institute in Nanjing (opened March 1928).

The 1956 simplified form (Part 3) completes the arc: a state sports body distilling a village fighting art into a five-minute public-health routine. When people say tai chi "is" a health exercise, they are describing something roughly seventy years old sitting on top of something roughly four hundred years old.

Self-studyThe name, and two things everyone gets wrong2 min read▶

太极拳 tàijíquán = 太 tài supreme + 极 jí ultimate or pole + 拳 quán fist or boxing. "Supreme Ultimate Boxing." The 拳 is not decoration - it marks this as a boxing method.

  • 太极 tàijí long predates the art. It is a cosmological term, appearing in the 繫辭 Xìcí commentary to the 易经 Yijing: 易有太极，是生两仪 - "the Changes has the Supreme Ultimate; this generates the two modes." So in English "tai chi" almost always means the exercise, while in Chinese 太极 alone means the concept and the exercise is 太极拳, with the 拳.
  • The "chi" in "tai chi" is not the "chi" in "chi energy." 极 jí and 气 qì are entirely different characters that happen to collide in the old Wade-Giles romanization ("t'ai chi ch'üan" versus "ch'i"). In pinyin, which is what medical and academic journals use, the collision disappears: taiji versus qi.
Part 2 · covers the internal / external split

"Internal art" - a phrase with a date on it 5 min live

Tai chi is always introduced as an 内家 nèijiā "internal" art, opposed to 外家 wàijiā "external" arts like Shaolin. That framing sounds timeless. It is not: it enters Chinese martial arts writing on a specific date, in a specific document, for reasons that are political rather than technical.

Live1669, and what the text actually says2.5 min▶

The first appearance is 1669, in 黄宗羲 Huang Zongxi's 《王征南墓志铭》, a tomb inscription for the boxer Wang Zhengnan (1617-1669).

✦ 王征南墓志铭 (1669)少林以拳勇名天下，然主於搏人，人亦得以乘之。有所謂內家者，以靜制動，犯者應手即仆，故別少林為外家。 "Shaolin is world-famous for boxing prowess, but it emphasises attacking, so an opponent can exploit that. There is what is called the internal school, which uses stillness to control movement; an assailant is felled the instant the hand meets him. Shaolin is therefore classed apart as the external school."

Two things to notice. First, this is a real and interesting technical idea - 以静制动, using stillness to control movement, is genuinely what tai chi trains. Second, and crucially: taijiquan is not named in this text. The document describes a Zhejiang lineage called the Internal School; the connection to tai chi was made retroactively, in the 20th century, partly by quietly substituting the well-known 王宗岳 Wang Zongyue for the obscure 王宗 Wang Zong who appears in the epitaph's lineage.

LiveWhy the split was political2 min▶

Stanley Henning's reading, now widely accepted: the internal/external division was an act of resistance, not a taxonomy. Huang Zongxi was a Ming loyalist writing under Manchu Qing rule. In Henning's words, "the 'external' school and Shaolin Monastery represented foreign Buddhism, which symbolized the Manchu aggressors, while the 'internal' school and Zhang Sanfeng represented indigenous Taoism." Huang even dated Wang's life by the sixty-year cycle rather than by Qing reign titles, avoiding any recognition of the dynasty. Henning calls the epitaph "an act of political defiance rather than one of technical classification."

The modern grouping is younger still. Tai chi, xingyi and bagua were bundled together as "the internal arts" at the Central Guoshu Institute in 1928, which organised its faculty into a Shaolin division and a Wudang division and sorted its national examination competitors into the same two boxes. Peter Lorge argues these practices existed as separate, unconnected traditions until an institution needed an "internal" category to set against Shaolin.

Douglas Wile's one-line history of the argument "For General Qi Jiguang in the Ming, the basic binary in martial arts was 'flowery' versus 'practical'; for Huang Zongxi in the Ming-Qing Transition, it was 'internal' versus 'external'; in the Republican Period, it was Shaolin versus Wudang; and today, it is 'traditional' versus 'modern.'" Every era gets the binary it needs. Knowing that makes you a much better consumer of martial arts marketing.
Part 3 · covers where it really came from

The legend, the retraction, and the documented chain 8 min live

The standard origin story: a Daoist immortal named 张三丰 Zhang Sanfeng invented tai chi after watching a snake fight a crane. It is a wonderful story. There is no historical support for it, and the best evidence against it comes from the very manuscripts that first made the claim.

LiveThe legend, and why scholars set it aside2.5 min▶

Douglas Wile, the leading Western scholar of the taiji classics, states the problem simply: "there is no record of a Zhang Sanfeng in the Song Dynasty, and there is no mention in the Ming histories of any connection between the immortal and the martial arts." Wile counts 81 different traditions of Zhang's creation of the art, placing him variously in the Song, Yuan or Ming, with three different ways of writing his name.

The snake and the crane have no verified pre-modern source at all. The oldest written Zhang Sanfeng martial origin, in the 1669 epitaph, is not an animal fight but a dream: 夜夢玄帝授之拳法, "that night in a dream, the Dark Emperor transmitted the boxing method to him." Wile files the animal story among folk narratives resting on "an immortal's dream, observation of a snake and stork, or the discovery of a secret manual in a cave, complete with fog machines and psychedelic light shows."

LiveThe 1867 claim and the 1881 retraction3 min▶

This is the single best thing in the historiography, and you can hold both documents in your head at once.

The earliest datable attribution of taijiquan to Zhang Sanfeng is 1867, in the manuscripts of 李亦畬 Li Yiyu (1832-1892), a member of the Yongnian family circle that produced the classics. It says: 太极拳始自宋张三丰 - "Taijiquan began with Zhang Sanfeng of the Song dynasty."

Fourteen years later, in the manuscript Li Yiyu handwrote and gave to his student 郝和 Hao He in 1881, the same man wrote:

✦ 李亦畬, 太极拳小序 (1881)太极拳不知始自何人，其精微巧妙，王宗岳论详且尽矣。 Tàijíquán bù zhī shǐ zì hé rén, qí jīngwēi qiǎomiào, Wáng Zōngyuè lùn xiáng qiě jìn yǐ. "I do not know with whom taijiquan began; its subtleties are exhaustively described in Wang Zongyue's treatise."

Same author. Same family. Fourteen years apart. Claim, then withdrawal. Nothing else demonstrates so cleanly that the origin story is a document with a history rather than a fact with a pedigree - and it is worth noticing that the retraction is the one written in his own hand for his own student.

Real world

The legend still won the marketplace. The first openly published work linking Zhang Sanfeng to taijiquan was 《太极拳经》 in 1912, about sixty years after tai chi reached Beijing, and the Yang-lineage books that followed carried what Wile calls "fabricated biographies of Zhang Sanfeng." Wile is blunt about why the argument stays hot: "For traditionalists, attacking the Zhang Sanfeng creation theory is tantamount to committing cultural treason," and "in today's martial arts marketplace, the various schools and styles vie to become commercial conquerors by inventing their histories."

LiveThe documented chain2.5 min▶

What survives scrutiny is a village, a family, and a student who left.

  • 陈家沟 Chen Village, Wen County, Henan, 17th century. UNESCO's own text says taijiquan "originated in mid-seventeenth century in Wenxian County, Henan Province." 陈王廷 Chen Wangting is credited as synthesiser. His dates are genuinely uncertain - three sets circulate - so say "seventeenth century" and move on.
  • His likely technical source was the Ming general 戚继光 Qi Jiguang's 《拳经》, a military boxing manual. The textual overlap was the key evidence in establishing this history.
  • 陈长兴 Chen Changxing (1771-1853) taught an outsider - 杨露禅 Yang Luchan (1799-1872), who began study in 1820, took the art home to Yongnian, and by 1850 was teaching the Imperial Guards' elite Palace Battalion in Beijing. Every style except Chen descends through him.
  • The scholar who established all this was 唐豪 Tang Hao, a lawyer turned martial arts historian, who published 《少林武当考》 in 1931 against both the Bodhidharma-Shaolin myth and the Zhang Sanfeng myth, then went to Chen Village to check the genealogy and interview elders. Tang: "Chinese martial arts were already highly developed in ancient times, and there is no reason to fabricate myths about Bodhidharma and Zhang Sanfeng." Wile's dry verdict: "Tang Hao's studies managed to anger both camps."
Still contested, and recently On 21 August 2007 China's General Administration of Sport gave Chen Village a plaque naming it "the birthplace of taijiquan." Two months later, protest forced the plaque's removal. This is a live argument, not a settled museum label.
Self-study1956, and the form most of the world does2 min read▶

In 1956 the State Physical Culture and Sports Commission commissioned the 二十四式简化太极拳, the simplified 24-form. It was built on Yang style, chosen for having 流传最广、适应性强 - the widest circulation and greatest adaptability - and designed to be 简单、明确、易学: simple, clear, easy to learn. Complex and repeated movements were cut. It runs about five to six minutes and became China's first unified martial arts textbook from a state sports authority.

It is almost certainly the most widely practised tai chi form in the world, though no rigorous count exists, so treat "most practiced" as a well-founded guess rather than a statistic.

Self-study2020: the myth loses at the UN1 min read▶

In December 2020, at the fifteenth session of its safeguarding committee, UNESCO inscribed "Taijiquan" - that exact name, not "Tai Chi" - on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity, submitted by China, as element 00424.

Read the official description closely and you find it endorses the seventeenth-century Wenxian origin. The international heritage listing follows Tang Hao's documented history, not the Daoist immortal. The myth lost at the UN.

Reflect & apply · 🧩 kit step 1

Start your readiness kit ✦ 10 min · builds all course

Your running project is a practice-readiness kit: the small set of things worth deciding before you walk into a room. Today it gets its first two entries - why you are interested, and an honest baseline you can re-check later.

Name which identity drew you. Martial art, health practice, or moving meditation? Write one sentence. There is no wrong answer, but the answer should steer which class you choose in Session 6 - and people who never ask themselves this are the ones who end up in the wrong room.

Record a balance baseline, safely. Stand near a wall or a sturdy chair, within arm's reach of support. Time how long you can stand on one leg, eyes open, without holding on. Stop at 30 seconds even if you could go longer, and stop immediately if you feel unsteady. Do both legs. Write the numbers down. Session 2 explains exactly what that test is measuring, and Session 5 tells you what the research does and does not conclude from it.

Write your one honest expectation - what you actually hope changes. Keep it concrete enough to check in three months. Session 5 will hold it against the evidence, kindly but squarely.

Safety note on the baseline test Support within reach, both hands free, no rush. If standing on one leg is not sensible for you today, that is useful information rather than a failure - note it, and note it again in Session 6 when you are choosing between a standing, seated or adapted class. Every good tai chi programme has all three.

Homework

Before Session 2 ◐ 20-30 min total

  • Watch five minutes of the 24-form online, from any source. You are not learning it. You are looking at one thing only: how the weight moves from foot to foot. Session 2 is entirely about that.
  • Notice your own weight shifts for one day. Standing at the kettle, waiting for a train - are you evenly on both feet, or settled onto one? No judgement, just notice. You will have a vocabulary for it after the next session.
  • Test the history claim in the wild. Find one tai chi school website and read its "history" page. Does it name Zhang Sanfeng as fact? Does it mention Chen Village? Neither answer makes it a bad school - but you can now read that page with a scholar's eye.
  • Optional deeper read: Douglas Wile, "Fighting Words," Martial Arts Studies 4 (2017). Open access, free, and the best single piece of writing on why tai chi history is so contested.
