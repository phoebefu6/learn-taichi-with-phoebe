Part 0

The session that ends with you in a room

Everything so far has been preparation for something you cannot do from a screen. You know what tai chi is and where it came from, you understand rooting well enough to know what a teacher is looking at when they stare at your back foot, you know what 松 sōng asks of you, you know the shape of the thirteen postures, and you know exactly what the evidence supports and what it does not. What you do not have is a form, and you are not going to get one here.

What you can get here is the last thing text is genuinely good for: how to choose well and start well. That turns out to matter more than it sounds, because tai chi has no certification standard, no regulator, and a traditional authority claim that a newcomer cannot check. In an unregulated market, knowing what to ask is the whole of your protection.

Part 1 · covers what you are looking at

The five families 8 min live

Style names are the first thing you meet on a class listing and the least useful thing on it. Here is the full picture anyway, because you should be able to read the listing - and because the story of how the five families relate to each other is the documented history from Session 1, told through people rather than dates.

StyleFounder and datesLineage inConventional characteristics

chén		 陈王廷 Chen Wangting, 17th century (dates genuinely uncertain - see Session 1) The oldest; originates and stays in Chen Village 缠丝劲 chánsījìn silk-reeling spiral force; alternating slow passages and explosive 发劲 fājìn; low stances. The most physically demanding.

Hedge the whole table, out loud Founders and dates are well attested. The characteristics column is not scholarship. Those descriptions come from lineage organisations and teaching tradition, not from peer-reviewed comparative study, and they vary between schools within a style as much as between styles. Two Yang classes in the same city can look very different. Treat the column as a rough orientation, and expect the actual room to override it.
LiveThe romanization trap - two families, one spelling3 min▶

This is the single most confusing thing in tai chi nomenclature, and almost every beginner is quietly wrong about it for years.

  • 吳 Wú - the family of 吴全佑 Wu Quanyou and 吴鉴泉 Wu Jianquan, descending from Yang Luchan and his son Yang Banhou. Second tone.
  • 武 Wǔ - the family of 武禹襄 Wu Yuxiang of 永年 Yongnian, the man from Session 1's manuscript story. Third tone.

Different character, different tone, different founder, different lineage, different body method. They are not branches of one thing. English spelling collapses them into "Wu style" and creates a genuine ambiguity, which is why the 武 style is conventionally written Wu(Hao) - the parenthetical honouring 郝为真 Hao Weizhen, who spread it. If a listing says "Wu style" with no character and no qualifier, you cannot tell which family it means from the words alone, and it is a perfectly reasonable thing to ask.

Live"Five" is a convention, not a closed set2 min▶

The five-family framing is standard, useful and slightly arbitrary. 和式 He style, descending from the 赵堡 Zhaobao tradition and named for 和兆元 He Zhaoyuan, is often counted as a sixth, and the Chinese Wushu Association's 段位 duànwèi grading syllabus covers all six - Chen, Yang, Wu, Wu(Hao), Sun and He. Within Chen there are further recognised frames: 老架 Old Frame from Chen Changxing, 小架 Small Frame from 陈有本 Chen Youben, and the Zhaobao line through 陈清平 Chen Qingping. 陈发科 Chen Fake (1887-1957) is the man who brought Chen style out of the village to Beijing.

None of this needs to be memorised. The point is simply that "the five styles" is a teaching convention that a tidy diagram makes look more settled than it is.

Real world

What to actually do with all of this. For a beginner, the style matters far less than the teacher and the class time. A good Yang teacher on a Tuesday evening ten minutes from your house beats a famous Chen teacher an hour away that you will attend four times. The only genuinely practical steers: Yang is the most widely available, so if you have one realistic option it is probably Yang. Chen is the most physically demanding, with low stances and explosive passages - a wonderful choice if that appeals and a poor first choice if your knees are the reason you came. Sun's high stances and following step suit people with limited knee range, and is worth seeking out specifically for that.

Part 2 · covers the credentialing vacuum

The certification reality 8 min live

Most people assume that somewhere there is a body that says who may teach tai chi. There is not. This is the most important paragraph in the session, and it is worth reading in the original.

NCCIH, verbatim "Tai chi instructors don't have to be licensed, and the practice isn't regulated by the Federal Government or individual states. There's no national standard for tai chi certification." NCCIH adds elsewhere that "various tai chi organizations offer training and certification programs with differing criteria." That is the whole legal position in the United States, stated by a federal health agency.
LiveWhat exists instead, and what each thing is actually worth4 min▶
  • IWUF, the International Wushu Federation. Real, international, and about sport. It governs competitive wushu, includes taijiquan as a competition event, and runs the World Taijiquan Championships. It licenses nothing about teaching. An IWUF connection tells you something about competitive pedigree and nothing whatever about competence with older or unwell students - which, for most people reading this, is the relevant competence.
  • Chinese Wushu Association 段位 duànwèi grading. A real system - nine duan across three bands, covering 23 styles, with 629 test centres according to 2013 secondary reporting, so treat those figures as dated and second-hand. It is domestic and sport-oriented, and crucially a Western beginner cannot easily verify a claimed duan. A number on a website that nobody can check is not a credential.
  • UK: the Tai Chi Union for Great Britain. Around 1,000 members and 800-plus instructors. Instructor applicants need at least five consecutive years of practice and are assessed on common principles rather than style specifics, which is a sensible design. The honest caveats: it is a voluntary membership body, not a statutory regulator; the assessment is a one-time panel plus an ongoing subscription rather than continuing supervision; and non-membership is not evidence of a bad teacher. It is a positive signal, not a filter.
  • US: no national body at all. The nearest analogue is programme-specific. The Tai Chi for Health Institute (Dr Paul Lam) certifies instructors in its own named programmes, requiring an authorised workshop, renewal every two years, and first aid and CPR. Scope caveat, stated fairly: this certifies delivery of a specific curriculum, not "qualified tai chi teacher" in general. But the renewal cycle and the CPR requirement are more accountability than most tai chi credentials carry anywhere, and that is worth knowing.
LiveOn lineage - the unfalsifiable credential3 min▶

Lineage is the traditional authority claim, and it fills exactly the space a licence would otherwise occupy. "I studied with X, who studied with Y, who was a disciple of Z" is doing the work that "registered with the state board" does in other fields.

Here is the structural problem, stated without any suggestion that lineage claims are usually false. Because no regulator exists, there is no register, no adjudicating body, and no penalty for overstating a connection. To a newcomer, a lineage claim is therefore unfalsifiable. You cannot check it, nobody will check it for you, and nothing happens to anyone who inflates it. A claim you cannot evaluate is not evidence, however true it happens to be.

Real world

Notice the silence. Neither NCCIH, nor Harvard Health, nor Mayo, nor the VA mentions lineage at all in its getting-started guidance. Not once, not to endorse it and not to warn about it. What they care about instead is safety, adaptation, and experience with your particular condition. Four independent health institutions writing about how to start tai chi, and none of them thinks who your teacher's teacher was belongs in the advice. That silence is itself the signal, and it should reset where you put your attention when you read a school's about page.

None of which means lineage is worthless. It is often a real marker of deep training, and a teacher who lights up when you ask about it is usually a teacher who loves the art. Ask about it as a conversation, not as a check.

Part 3 · covers how to choose

How to actually vet a teacher 7 min live

There is a vetting checklist endorsed by an actual health authority, and almost nobody in the tai chi world uses it: NCCIH's "6 Things To Know When Selecting a Complementary Health Practitioner." Its key item is not the one you would guess.

LiveThe question that outranks certification3 min▶

NCCIH's central instruction is not "ask what certification they hold." It is: explain all your health conditions, and ask about the practitioner's training and experience working with people who have your conditions. In NCCIH's words: "Choose a practitioner who understands how to work with people with your specific needs, even if general well-being is your goal."

Read that last clause twice. Even if general well-being is your goal. The advice is not aimed only at people with a diagnosis. It is saying that the useful thing to know about a teacher is who they have successfully taught, not what they have collected.

The rest of the checklist, adapted:

  • Get names through your doctor or a local hospital rather than only through search. Hospitals and community health services often run or refer to classes, and those classes have already passed somebody's safety bar.
  • Ask whether they will work alongside your conventional providers. The answer is a character test as much as a logistics question.
  • Tell all of your providers what you are doing. This is the item people skip, and it costs nothing.

Better proxies than a certificate, given that certificates mean so little here: a credential with a renewal cycle plus a CPR requirement; willingness to adapt movements or offer a seated option; willingness to coordinate with your doctor; and recognition of the programme in a federal evidence tier - NCOA publishes which tai chi programmes qualify as evidence-based falls prevention, and that list is checkable in a way that a lineage claim is not.

Self-studyReading the red flags fairly2 min read▶

Two of the red flags deserve a note, because they are the ones people over-apply.

"Lineage claim as the main credential" is a red flag only when it is doing all the work. A teacher who names their teacher and then talks about how they adapt for arthritic knees is fine. A teacher whose entire about page is a chain of names and no mention of who they teach or how is telling you where their attention goes.

"Dismisses questions about the history" is not a demand that every teacher be a scholar. Plenty of excellent teachers happily say "the Zhang Sanfeng story is what we were told, I have never looked into it" - that is a perfectly good answer and it is honest. The flag is for irritation at being asked, which tends to travel with irritation at being asked other things too.

Conversely, the strongest green flag on the list is the plainest one: they let you watch a class before joining. A teacher confident enough to be observed by a stranger is usually a teacher worth observing.

Part 4 · covers what actually happens

What the first weeks actually look like 7 min live

Most people who quit tai chi quit in the first month, and a good share of them quit because reality did not match an expectation nobody corrected. So here is the reality, sourced.

LiveThe shape of a class, and what to wear3 min▶

Harvard Health describes the standard structure: warm-up, then form instruction, then qigong breathing. Sixty minutes is the research standard, and Harvard notes that "most beginning programs and tai chi interventions tested in medical research last at least 12 weeks, with instruction once or twice a week and practice at home."

  • Loose clothing. Nothing that restricts the knee or hip.
  • Barefoot, or thin flexible shoes - soles thin enough that you can feel the ground. This is the detail almost nobody tells beginners and it matters more than the clothing. Running shoes are "usually unsuitable": thick cushioning blunts the ground feel that Session 2 was entirely about, and a raised heel tips your weight forward and interferes with the weight-shifting you are there to learn. If you turn up in trainers, expect a class where you can feel less of what the teacher is describing.
  • No equipment. Nothing to buy before you start, which is one of the genuinely lovely things about this practice.
  • Seated and wheelchair variants are normal. The VA runs and publishes them. Asking for one is not asking for a favour.
  • 推手 tuīshǒu push hands is not standard beginner content. If a beginner class advertises push hands, that is not a warning sign, but it does tell you the teacher's centre of gravity is the martial identity from Session 1. Harvard's typical class does not include it.
LiveThe timeline anchor - and it is a good one3 min▶

Every commercial claim about how fast you can learn tai chi runs into one very stubborn fact.

Tai Ji Quan: Moving for Better Balance (TJQMBB) is the CDC, ACL and NCOA-recognised top-tier falls prevention programme from Session 5. It is derived from the Simplified 24-Form, and it was cut down to an eight-form core. That eight-movement curriculum is delivered as two one-hour sessions weekly for 24 weeks.

★ Sit with the arithmetic Researchers who needed a programme that actually worked took the 24-form, reduced it to eight movements, and still allocated 24 weeks. So "learn tai chi in 8 weeks" is not a lie exactly - it describes memorising a sequence, which is a real and achievable thing. It does not describe acquiring a skill. Knowing the difference before you start is worth more than any amount of enthusiasm.

For comparison: Tai Chi for Arthritis and Fall Prevention runs either 8 weeks with two sessions a week or 16 weeks with one - 16 hour-long sessions in total either way. That is a realistic dose for a structured beginner programme, and notice how modest it is next to the number of hours people imagine they need.

You will also hear that the 24-form takes 8 to 12 weeks to learn. Hedge it. That figure comes from school and commercial sources only; no institutional or peer-reviewed source states a learning duration. Call it practitioner consensus, and pair it with the TJQMBB numbers whenever anyone quotes it at you.

★ The reassuring part, and it is properly sourced Every single trial in Session 5 measured real outcomes at 12 to 24 weeks in participants who had learned only abbreviated material. The Cochrane falls reduction, the fibromyalgia result, the Parkinson's balance improvement, the blood pressure change - none of them required anyone to complete a form. You do not need to finish a form to get the documented benefit. If you were quietly worried that the good stuff only arrives after years, the evidence says otherwise, and it says so from the same trials that produced all of Session 5's numbers.

Free resources worth using, before and after 4 min live

All of these were verified live for this course. All of them are free. They are ordered roughly by how useful they are on the day before your first class.

LiveStart here, and take one with you2 min▶
  • NCCIH, "Tai Chi: What You Need To Know." The best single starting point anywhere, and short.
  • NCCIH, "Qigong." Because a chunk of your first class will be qigong breathing and nobody will explain what that word means.
  • NCCIH, "6 Things To Know When Selecting a Complementary Health Practitioner." This is the one to actually take with you. It is the checklist Part 3 is built on.
  • VA Whole Health tai chi and qigong pages, plus VA News, "Live Whole Health #300: Cloud Hands" - an 18-minute follow-along led by a VA recreation therapist and certified instructor, and the best free government-produced tai chi video found for this course. Also "#253: Adaptive Tai Chi" for the seated version.
  • Harvard Health free articles: "The Health Benefits of Tai Chi" and "The active ingredients of tai chi" - the second is Peter Wayne's eight ingredients from Session 5.
  • NCOA programme pages: TJQMBB, Tai Chi for Arthritis, and Evidence-Based Falls Prevention Programs. This is where you check whether a local programme is in a federal evidence tier.
  • For the history: Douglas Wile, "Fighting Words," Martial Arts Studies 4 (2017), open access; and Brennan Translation for the bilingual taiji classics, including the 1881 Li Yiyu manuscript. UNESCO element 00424 for the official description.
Explicitly do not use these two The NHS tai chi guide has been withdrawn. Its old URL now 301-redirects to a generic exercise hub that contains zero occurrences of the phrase "tai chi"; the last archive snapshot is 2022-03-08. Third-party sites still quoting "the NHS says" about tai chi are quoting stale copies of withdrawn content. Harvard's "An Introduction to Tai Chi" is a paid $29 report, frequently mis-cited as free - the Harvard Health articles listed above are the free ones. The meta-lesson is worth more than either correction: even good institutional sources go stale. That is exactly why this course dates its citations, and why you should check the date on anything you are about to trust.
Reflect & apply · 🧩 final kit assembly

Assemble the whole kit ✦ 15 min · finishes the project

Five sessions of small entries now become one document. Pull the earlier pieces together, add the three new sections, and you have something you can genuinely take into a phone call with a stranger who runs a class.

Gather the four earlier entries. Your why and balance baseline from Session 1, your rooting self-tests from Session 2, your posture checklist from Session 3, your style shortlist from Session 4. Put them in one place, unedited, with their original dates.

Add the evidence-honest expectation from Session 5. With its certainty rating and its comparator. This is the entry that keeps you truthful with yourself in three months.

Write the three questions you will ask a prospective teacher. One of them should be the NCCIH question about experience with people who have your conditions. The other two are yours - about adaptation, about class size, about whether you can watch first, about what a first month looks like. Write them down, because on the phone you will forget.

Name your chosen first class, and the reason. Not the best class in your city. The one you will actually attend on a Tuesday. Write the reason next to it, because the reason is what you will re-read in week five.

Write your "what I will judge at week 12, not week 2" line. One sentence. Weeks one and two are entirely about feeling clumsy in a room full of people who are not clumsy, and that tells you nothing about whether tai chi is for you. Deciding in advance when you will evaluate is the single most useful thing on this page.

Homework

The last one is just: go ◐ two weeks

  • Contact two classes from your shortlist and ask your three questions. Email is fine. Notice which one answers the health-experience question with a real answer and which one answers with a certificate.
  • Watch one class before joining, if they allow it. Watch the feet, not the arms - Session 2 earned you that. Watch what the teacher does when someone gets it wrong, which is the single most informative thirty seconds available to you.
  • Go. That is the homework. Everything in this course was written so that this step would be less intimidating, and there is nothing left to read that will make you readier than turning up.
  • Then wait twelve weeks before you decide anything. You already wrote the line committing to this. Keep it.
The honest closing note If you go for twelve weeks and it is not for you, Session 5 already gave you permission. Other forms of exercise do about as well for most outcomes, the Cochrane review is quite clear that balance and functional exercise slightly outperforms tai chi on fall rate, and there is no obligation buried anywhere in this course. The point was never loyalty to a tradition. It was to make sure that if you walked away, you walked away knowing what you were walking away from - which is a very different thing from drifting off in week three because nobody told you about the shoes.
