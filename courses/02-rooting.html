If you take one thing from this course into a class, take this. Almost everything tai chi does with the legs is a consequence of two facts about balance and one classical instruction about not being stuck. Today you get all three, and a lab where you can watch the geometry work.

Part 0

Why this session exists

Beginners watch a tai chi class and see arms. Teachers watch the same class and see feet. The gap between those two views is what this session closes. Rooting is not a mystical property you acquire after years - it is a describable relationship between where your weight is and where your feet are, and you can understand it completely in forty-five minutes even though you will spend years refining it in your body.

Part 1 · covers the mechanics of standing

Two facts, and everything follows 6 min live

Standing balance runs on two quantities. Your base of support is the area on the ground enclosed by whatever is touching it - for two feet, roughly the region spanning both footprints and the space between. Your centre of mass is the single point where your body's weight effectively acts, sitting somewhere around your lower belly when you stand.

LiveWhat "rooting" actually names3 min▶

You will hear rooting described as sinking energy into the ground, or growing roots like a tree. Those are the tradition's images and they are good teaching images - but underneath them sits something plainly describable: keeping your centre of mass well inside your base of support, keeping it low, and knowing at every moment which foot is carrying you.

The classics say it in a line about the chain of movement, which circulates through several of the canonical texts:

✦ From the classics其根在脚，发于腿，主宰于腰，形于手指。 Qí gēn zài jiǎo, fā yú tuǐ, zhǔzǎi yú yāo, xíng yú shǒuzhǐ. "The root is in the feet, issued through the legs, governed by the waist, expressed in the fingers."

A note on honesty: that line moves between texts - it appears within Wu Yuxiang's writings and also inside editions attributed to Zhang Sanfeng - so attribute it to the classics generally rather than to one named author. Session 4 explains why that kind of hedging is necessary throughout this literature.

Real world

Why teachers seem obsessed with your feet. If the root is in the feet and everything else is downstream, then a fault in the legs propagates upward and cannot be fixed at the arms. This is why a good teacher will ignore your beautiful hand shape and tell you your back knee is collapsing. They are not being pedantic; they are working at the only place the problem can actually be solved.

Live双重 shuāngzhòng - the classical fault3 min▶

Wang Zongyue's 太极拳论, the most quoted text in the tradition, gives the whole doctrine of weighting in eight characters:

✦ 太极拳论 · the eight characters偏沉则随，双重则滞。 Piān chén zé suí, shuāng zhòng zé zhì. "Sink to one side and you can follow; be doubly weighted and you stagnate."

That is the entire classical statement. 双重 shuāngzhòng, "double weighting," is named as a fault - not because even weight is unstable, but because it is stuck. If both legs are loaded, neither can move without first unloading, and that extra beat is the one your partner uses.

Yang Chengfu makes the same point in his fourth essential, 分虚实 fēn xū shí, "distinguish empty and full," which he calls the number one rule: shift onto one leg, that leg is 实 shí solid, the other is 虚 xū empty, and only then are turns "light, nimble and almost without effort."

Self-study沉 chén, sinking, and what it buys2 min read▶

Sinking runs all through the Ten Essentials you meet properly in Session 3. Essential 2, 含胸拔背, links containing the chest to 气沉丹田 - sinking the breath to the lower belly. Essential 5 is 沉肩坠肘, sinking the shoulders and dropping the elbows.

Mechanically, sinking lowers your centre of mass without changing your footprint, and Fact 2 says that buys you more recoverable lean from exactly the same feet. You can watch that number move in the lab in a moment. It is one of the places where a traditional instruction and ordinary physics say precisely the same thing, and it is worth noticing that they do - not every instruction in this tradition has that property.

Part 2 · the build-along · everyone experiments

The rooting lab ✦ 20 min hands-on

This is a real balance model seen from above. The dashed outline is a convex hull computed from the actual foot polygons; the dot is tested against it with real geometry; and the recoverable-lean figure is atan(margin ÷ height of your centre of mass), which is ordinary physics rather than a tai chi claim. Drag the slider and watch what the classics are describing.

Start in 无极 wuji, the parallel stance, weight even. Look at the margin figure - about 12.5 cm, the best number you will see today. Now look at the verdict. The lab flags 双重, double-weighted, because both feet are loaded. Maximum stability, zero mobility. That is the classical fault, measured.

Slide the weight across. Watch two numbers move in opposite directions: the margin shrinks as your centre travels toward one foot, and at around 90/10 the lighter foot goes white - it is free to step. You are buying mobility with stability. That trade is what 分虚实 is asking you to make, and no amount of practice abolishes it.

Switch to 弓步, the bow stance. Notice the base is now long and narrow rather than square. Front-to-back you have far more room than in the parallel stance; side-to-side, less. This is why the bow stance is the workhorse of the form, which travels forward and back far more than it travels sideways.

Push the slider to either extreme. The dot turns red and leaves the base entirely: off your root. Notice how little travel it took. In a real body this is the moment you either take an unplanned step or fall, and everything tai chi teaches about not over-committing is about staying away from this edge.

Turn on 沉 sink. The margin does not change - same feet, same footprint - but the recoverable lean rises from 7.1° to 8.1° in the parallel stance. That is the entire mechanical payoff of sinking, and it is real.

Try 虚步, the empty stance, and load the front foot. Watch the margin collapse. That front foot is a feeler, not a pillar; putting weight on a toe-sized contact patch is exactly the beginner error the stance is designed to train out.

Finish on 独立, single leg. The smallest base in the course, roughly 4.5 cm of margin, about 2.6° of recoverable lean. Compare that to your one-leg baseline time from Session 1 and you have a sense of what the golden-rooster postures are actually asking of you.

An honesty note about the numbers on this page The percentages are modern pedagogy, not classical doctrine. No classical text specifies 70/30, 60/40 or 100/0 - Wang Zongyue's treatise says only 偏沉则随，双重则滞, and even the explicit contrast between 双重 and "single-weighting" 单重 belongs to a later manuscript layer, the Yang family 《太极法说》 of around 1875. The ratios you will hear in class are teaching conventions that vary by school and even within a style: Yang lineages commonly say roughly 70/30 in the bow stance, Wu lineages often work much closer to 100/0. The lab uses percentages because they make the trade-off visible on a screen, not because a 17th-century boxer was counting them.
Part 3 · covers what the model buys you

Five things the lab just explained 5 min live

The value of understanding the geometry is that a dozen apparently arbitrary class instructions turn out to be the same instruction.

LiveWhy the form moves so slowly1.5 min▶

Because the interesting part is the transition. Any competent adult can stand in a bow stance; the skill is in travelling between stances without the centre of mass straying near an edge. Slow movement is not a stylistic choice or a relaxation technique - it is the speed at which you can still notice and correct where your weight is. Speed hides errors, and the form is built to expose them.

LiveWhy teachers say "empty the foot before you move it"1.5 min▶

Because a loaded foot cannot move without a lurch. In the lab, the lighter foot only turns white and becomes free below about 10% load. If you try to step with 40% still on that foot, your body must first dump that weight somewhere - and that dumping motion is the visible wobble every beginner produces and every teacher spots instantly. Empty first, then move. The order is the whole instruction.

LiveWhy the stances are wide, and why they are not wider1 min▶

A wider stance is a bigger base, which is more margin. But a bigger base also means further to travel to unload a foot, and a very wide stance strands you in double weighting because neither foot can empty easily. The stances of the form sit at a compromise: wide enough for real margin, narrow enough that a weight shift can still free a foot in one movement. Try the extremes in the lab and the compromise becomes obvious.

Self-studyWhy this generalises past tai chi2 min read▶

Everything above is true of walking up stairs, carrying shopping, and getting out of a chair. That is not a coincidence, and it is a large part of why tai chi shows up in falls-prevention research at all - a practice that spends an hour a week rehearsing controlled weight transfer near the edge of the base of support is, structurally, a falls-prevention intervention whether or not anyone designed it as one.

Session 5 gives that idea its numbers, including the honest finding that other kinds of balance exercise do about as well.

Reflect & apply · 🧩 kit step 2

Your rooting self-tests ✦ 8 min · builds your kit

Three small physical checks, all done with support within arm's reach. These are observations, not exercises - you are gathering a description of where you actually are, so that Session 6's teacher conversation is grounded in something real.

The shift test. Stand feet shoulder-width, weight even. Slowly move your weight fully onto your left foot until your right foot is light enough to lift a centimetre off the floor. Note whether you had to lean, whether your torso stayed upright, and how far your hip travelled sideways. Repeat right. Most people discover they lean rather than shift.

The empty-foot test. From that same position, with the right foot light, hold for five seconds and see whether the foot can genuinely stay unweighted or keeps drifting back down for support. A foot that keeps touching down is telling you the shift is not complete.

The sinking test. Stand tall, then soften the knees a few centimetres without tipping forward. Notice what your thighs report, and whether your breathing changed. Tai chi is done in this softened range continuously, which is why an hour of it is more work than it looks.

Nothing here should hurt Support within reach for all three. Knees soften over the feet, not collapsing inward. If any of it produces knee pain, stop - and note it, because "which movements bother my knees" is one of the most useful things you can tell a prospective teacher in Session 6.

Homework

Before Session 3 ◐ 5 min a day

  • Shift practice, one minute a day. Feet shoulder-width, support nearby, slowly move your weight left and right, aiming to keep the torso upright rather than leaning. This is genuinely the highest-value minute available to you before a first class.
  • Notice double weighting in ordinary life. Standing in a queue, at a counter - are both feet loaded? Can you feel that you would need an extra beat to move? That noticing is the skill.
  • Return to the lab once and try to predict the margin before you read it. Getting a feel for how quickly it collapses is worth more than memorising any number.
  • Optional: watch the 24-form again, and this time watch only the transitions between postures rather than the postures themselves. It is a different video the second time.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · 双重 shuāngzhòng, double weighting, is a fault because...

Even weight is actually the most stable position - it has the biggest margin. The problem is mobility: 偏沉则随，双重则滞, "sink to one side and you can follow; be doubly weighted and you stagnate."

2 · Sinking 沉 improves your balance because...

The footprint does not change - the margin figure stays identical in the lab. What changes is the angle you can lean and still come back, because the recoverable lean is atan(margin / height).

3 · The 70/30 weight ratio you will hear in class is...

The classics give only 偏沉则随，双重则滞. Ratios vary by school - Yang lineages often teach roughly 70/30, Wu lineages often much closer to 100/0. Useful conventions, not doctrine.
