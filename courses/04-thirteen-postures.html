Part 0

Why this session exists

Watch a form and you see an endless procession of named postures - grasp the sparrow's tail, single whip, cloud hands, on and on - and it looks like something you would have to memorise for years before it made any sense. Underneath, it is much smaller than that. There are eight ways of meeting an incoming force and five ways of moving, and the postures are combinations of them. Eight plus five is thirteen, which is why 十三势 shí sān shì, "the thirteen postures," is also an older name for the whole art. Today: the set itself, the honest status of the cosmological overlays that get printed on top of it, and what push hands is actually for.

Part 1 · covers the eight ways of meeting force

八门 the eight gates 8 min live

八门 bā mén, "eight gates," is the tradition's inventory of ways a body can meet an incoming force. They are not eight techniques in the sense of eight moves to memorise - they are eight qualities, any of which can show up inside almost any posture. UNESCO's own description of taijiquan uses exactly this framing of five steps and eight techniques.

LiveThe eight, with plain English4 min▶

Read the primaries first. They are the four you will meet in every beginner form, usually bundled into the sequence English speakers call "grasp the sparrow's tail."

汉字PinyinNamePlain EnglishGroup
掤péngward-offbuoyant outward support - the quality that keeps a released body from going flat四正 primary
捋lǚroll-backleading an incoming force past you rather than stopping it四正 primary
挤jǐpresssqueezing forward, usually with one arm backed by the other四正 primary
按ànpush, press downa downward and forward pressing with the palms四正 primary
采cǎiplucka sharp downward grab that pulls the other person's balance with it四隅 corner
挒lièsplit, rendtwo opposing forces applied at once, in different directions四隅 corner
肘zhǒuelbowusing the elbow, at close range where the hand no longer reaches四隅 corner
靠kàoshoulder, body bumpusing the shoulder or trunk, at the closest range of all四隅 corner

Notice the progression built into the corner four: 采 and 挒 use the hands and arms, 肘 uses the elbow, 靠 uses the body. They are ordered by how close you have been allowed to get. That is a piece of martial logic sitting quietly inside a list most people learn as vocabulary.

Real world

What this buys you in week one. A beginner watching a form sees forty unrelated shapes. Someone who knows the eight gates sees the same 掤 quality showing up in a dozen different postures with different arm positions. You are not learning forty things; you are learning eight things in forty arrangements. That reframe alone makes the first months considerably less discouraging.

LiveThe 掤 pronunciation trap2 min▶

Worth knowing before you look it up and get confused. 掤 is read péng only inside taijiquan jargon. Open a Chinese dictionary and the character is bīng, meaning a quiver lid - the cover on an arrow case. It has no everyday martial meaning at all.

This is not a modern internet discovery. Cheng Man-ching noted exactly this: the reading used throughout the art is a specialist convention rather than the standard one. So if a dictionary app tells you the word is bīng, the app is right and the school is also right, and the two facts live comfortably together as long as you know which register you are in.

Label it as tradition holds, not the text says. It is a convention of the community, and a very stable one - but it is a convention.

Self-study掤 péng and 松 sōng, revisited2 min read▶

Session 3 introduced 掤 as the partner of 松: the buoyant outward supportive quality that keeps a released body from collapsing. Here it turns up again, this time as the first of the eight gates, which tells you something about how the tradition organises itself.

掤 is doing double duty. It is a technique - a specific way of meeting an incoming force with a rounded, supported arm structure. And it is a quality that is supposed to be present continuously, in every posture, whether or not you are doing the technique called ward-off. That double meaning is why teachers say things like "you have lost your péng" while you are doing something that is plainly not ward-off. They mean the support has gone out of you, not that you performed the wrong move.

The floating-arms test from Session 3's audit is the cheapest way to feel the quality version. The technique version needs a partner and a teacher, and this course is not going to pretend otherwise.

Part 2 · covers the five ways of moving

五步 the five steps 6 min live

The other half of the thirteen. Four of them are what you would expect from a list of ways to move. The fifth is the interesting one, and it is the one that connects this session straight back to Session 2's lab.

Live中定 is not the fifth direction3 min▶

The list reads 进 jìn advance, 退 tuì retreat, 左顾 zuǒ gù look left, 右盼 yòu pàn gaze right, 中定 zhōng dìng central equilibrium. Four of those are movements. The fifth is a category error if you read it as a movement, and reading it correctly is the point of this part.

中定 is not a direction you go in. It is the state the other four move around. You do not do 中定 instead of advancing; you advance while maintaining it, or you fail to and the step becomes a lurch. It is the condition that makes the other four survivable.

And you have already measured it. Session 2's lab computed a base of support from the foot polygons and tested whether the centre of mass stayed inside it, reporting a margin in centimetres and a recoverable lean in degrees. That margin is 中定, quantified. The lab was not a warm-up for this session - it was this session's fifth step, three weeks early, with numbers on it.

Real world

Why this is the practical half of the thirteen. Almost nothing in a beginner health class is about the eight gates - you will not be doing 挒 or 靠 to anyone. But every single transition in the form is an exercise in the five steps, and specifically in keeping 中定 while doing the other four. If you take one thing from this session into a class, take the habit of asking, mid-transition, whether your centre is still where you can recover it.

Self-studyWhy 左顾 and 右盼 use looking words2 min read▶

顾 gù and 盼 pàn both mean to look, to glance, to attend to - not to step. That is worth a moment, because it means two of the five "steps" are not primarily about the feet at all. They name where your attention and orientation go, with the body turn following.

This lines up with 用意不用力 from Session 3, and with the plain-English gloss given there: lead the movement with attention rather than muscular push. The five steps encode the same priority in their vocabulary - two footwork terms, two attention terms, and one state.

A caution, though: this is an interpretive reading of the wording, not something the classics spell out. Different schools weight the looking and the stepping differently, and some teach 左顾 and 右盼 as straightforward sideways footwork. Hold it lightly.

Part 3 · the honesty beat

The correspondences are overlays, not doctrine 8 min live

Almost every account of the thirteen postures pairs them with the eight trigrams of the 易经 and the five phases 金木水火土. The pairing is genuinely old and it is genuinely in the classics. It is also genuinely unstable - the sources disagree with each other, in two separate ways, and both disagreements are live.

LiveWhat the 1881 manuscript actually says2.5 min▶

The text says. The best primary witness is Li Yiyu's handwritten manual of 1881, the same manuscript that carries the "I do not know with whom taijiquan began" line from Session 1:

✦ 李亦畬 manuscript (1881)掤捋擠按。即坎離震兑。四正方也。 採挒肘靠。即乾坤艮巽。四斜角也。 進步退步左顧右盻中定。即金木水火土也。 "Peng, lü, ji, an: these are kan, li, zhen, dui - the four cardinal directions. Cai, lie, zhou, kao: these are qian, kun, gen, xun - the four diagonal corners. Advance, retreat, look left, gaze right, central equilibrium: these are metal, wood, water, fire, earth."

So the structure of the mapping is clear enough: four primaries to the four cardinal trigrams, four corner methods to the four corner trigrams, five steps to the five phases. If the story ended there, this would be a tidy correspondence table and nobody would need a hedge.

LiveDiscrepancy 1 · which trigram goes where2.5 min▶

The 1881 text gives 掤=坎, 捋=離, 挤=震, 按=兑 for the primaries, and the corner trigrams 乾坤艮巽 for the corner methods. Many modern sources give a completely different assignment - commonly 掤=乾, 捋=坤 and so on, putting the corner trigrams on the primary techniques and vice versa.

This is not sloppiness on anyone's part. The difference tracks a real distinction in Chinese cosmology: 先天八卦 xiāntiān bāguà, the pre-heaven arrangement, versus 后天八卦 hòutiān bāguà, the post-heaven arrangement. These are two standard orderings of the same eight trigrams around a circle, and they place the trigrams in different compass positions. Map the eight gates onto compass positions and then read off the trigrams, and which arrangement you used decides the answer.

There is no single canonical mapping. Both are defensible; both have textual support somewhere; the schools that teach each are equally confident. Anyone who tells you their version is the correct one is telling you which arrangement their lineage inherited, not settling a question.

LiveDiscrepancy 2 · the five phases, and the version everyone teaches3 min▶

This one is sharper, because here the version almost universally taught is not the one the 1881 classic gives.

Read the classic positionally - pair the five steps with the five phases in the order they are listed - and you get:

  • 进 advance = 金 metal · 退 retreat = 木 wood · 左顾 look left = 水 water · 右盼 gaze right = 火 fire · 中定 = 土 earth

Now open the 《清代杨氏传抄老谱》, the Qing-dynasty Yang-family transcribed manuscript, and you get a different mapping built on directional cosmology rather than list order:

  • 进 advance = 火 fire, south · 退 retreat = 水 water, north · 左顾 = 木 wood, east · 右盼 = 金 metal, west · 中定 = 土 earth, centre

The second version is coherent - it uses the standard phase-to-direction assignments that run through all of Chinese correlative thought, the same ones a TCM course would teach you. It is also the version almost everyone teaches, including schools that will cite the 1881 manuscript as their authority on the same page.

The honest line, worth memorising The eight-gate and five-step structure is stable and old. The trigram and five-phase correspondences are mnemonic overlays that vary between texts and schools, and should be taught as such. That is not a debunking - the overlays are part of the tradition and they are useful as memory aids and as a way of saying "these eight belong together." It is a statement about what kind of thing they are. Structure: load-bearing. Overlay: decorative and variable.
Self-studyIf you took the TCM course, you have seen this before2 min read▶

The pattern here is not specific to tai chi. It is the correspondence-system habit that runs through classical Chinese thought: take any set of things, count them, and if the count is five or eight, map them onto the five phases or the eight trigrams.

The habit is generative - it produces elegant, memorable, internally consistent systems - and it is also unconstrained, because nothing stops a later writer from producing a different consistent mapping of the same set. That is precisely what happened here, twice.

The reading strategy is the same one that course recommended: treat the correspondence as a filing system the tradition uses to organise and remember its own material, and do not treat agreement between two systems as evidence that either is describing a mechanism. When two mappings of the same eight items disagree, that is not a scandal. It is what you should expect from a filing system with more than one librarian.

Part 4 · covers what partner work is for

推手 push hands: listening, not sparring 10 min live

推手 tuīshǒu, "push hands," is where the eight gates stop being vocabulary. It is also the single most misunderstood thing in tai chi from the outside, where it gets read as either sparring or theatre. It is neither. It is a sensitivity exercise, and UNESCO lists it as a core component of the practice.

LiveWhat it is, in the words of people who study it3 min▶

Research says. An academic definition, from Gómez-Lozano and colleagues in Frontiers in Communication 2022;7:983290:

✦ Gómez-Lozano et al, Frontiers in Communication 2022;7:983290"The aim of Tuishou practice is to sensitize oneself to an opponent's movement and intention while concealing one's own." Non-competitive tuishou, in the same paper: "a collaborative somatic listening application"

Read "collaborative somatic listening" slowly, because every word is doing work. Collaborative: you and your partner are building something together, not competing. Somatic: the information channel is touch, not sight. Listening: the goal is to receive information, not to deliver force.

The tradition names three skills, in order of difficulty:

  • 听劲 tīng jìn, listening energy - detecting what the other person is doing through contact. It comes first because nothing else is possible without it.
  • 化劲 huà jìn, transforming energy - redirecting what you detected, rather than blocking it.
  • 懂劲 dǒng jìn, understanding energy - the mature skill, where you know what is coming before it fully arrives.

And a four-character strategy that governs the contact itself: 粘连黏随 zhān lián nián suí - stick, connect, adhere, follow. All four are about not losing contact and not fighting it. Nothing in that phrase is about winning.

LiveExpectation setting: you probably will not do this2 min▶

An honest warning, because it saves disappointment on both sides.

Push hands is not sparring, and it is not standard beginner content. Harvard Health's description of a typical tai chi class runs warm-up, then form instruction, then qigong breathing. Push hands is not mentioned. Most health-oriented classes, which is most classes, never do it at all, and many teachers who do teach it hold it back for students who already have a form.

So: do not expect it in week one, do not judge a class for not offering it, and do not assume a class that does offer it is more advanced or more authentic. It is a different curriculum choice, and Session 6 will help you work out which curriculum you actually want. What today gives you is the ability to ask the question intelligently.

LiveThe classics on sensitivity, and the clause everyone drops2.5 min▶

The text says. The most famous statement of the sensitivity ideal, from 太极拳论:

✦ 太极拳论一羽不能加，蝇虫不能落。 Yī yǔ bù néng jiā, yíng chóng bù néng luò. "A single feather cannot be added; a fly or insect cannot alight."

Quoted alone, as it usually is, it sounds like a boast about superhuman touch. But the very next clause completes the thought and changes what kind of claim it is:

✦ 太极拳论 · the completing clause人不知我，我独知人。 Rén bù zhī wǒ, wǒ dú zhī rén. "The opponent does not know me; I alone know the opponent."

Put them back together and the logic is plain. The invisibility claim rests on the sensitivity claim. You are not unreadable because you are hiding; you are unreadable because you are reading faster than they are, so you are always responding to them rather than committing to something they can feel. Drop the second clause and the first becomes a mystical flourish. Keep it and it is an argument.

Live四两拨千斤 - the most-mangled line in English tai chi writing3 min▶

"Four ounces moves a thousand pounds." You have seen it. It is on posters, in book titles, in the first paragraph of a thousand articles, and it is nearly always attributed to Wang Zongyue's 太极拳论. Three things about it are wrong, and all three are fixable.

1. It is not from the Treatise. 四两拨千斤 is from 《打手歌》, the "Song of Striking Hands." The Treatise quotes it, as an existing saying:

✦ 太极拳论, quoting an existing line察四两拨千斤之句，显非力胜。 Chá sì liǎng bō qiān jīn zhī jù, xiǎn fēi lì shèng. "Examining the line 'four taels deflect a thousand catties,' it is clear this is not victory by strength."

The decisive word is 句 jù, "the line". You do not write "examining the line X" about a sentence you are in the middle of composing. The Treatise is citing something its readers already knew. So the attribution people give it is not just imprecise, it is backwards.

2. The full form has two more characters, and they are the mechanism. The line in 打手歌 reads 牵动四两拨千斤 qiān dòng sì liǎng bō qiān jīn - "draw him into motion, and four taels deflect a thousand catties." 牵动 qiān dòng means to pull, to tug into movement, to set in motion.

This is not decoration and it is not a preamble. Without first pulling the opponent off balance, four taels deflect nothing. The tiny force works on someone already travelling, already outside their own base - which is exactly the condition Session 2's lab put a number on. Strip 牵动 out and the line becomes a claim that a feather can move a boulder, which is silly. Leave it in and it becomes a claim about timing and balance, which is correct.

3. The units are wrong in the standard translation. 两 liǎng is a tael, not an ounce, and 斤 jīn is a catty, not a pound. 1 jin = 16 liang, so the ratio the line states is 1:4,000. And a late-Qing jin weighed about 596.8 g, so what the line literally says is four taels (about 150 g) deflect a thousand catties (about 600 kg). "Four ounces moves a thousand pounds" is right in spirit and wrong in units, and the real weights are a good deal more impressive.

The line, restored 牵动四两拨千斤 - from 《打手歌》, not the Treatise. "Draw him into motion, and four taels deflect a thousand catties." About 150 g against about 600 kg, and only after the drawing. The 牵动 is the whole thing.
Self-studyWhy Session 2 already showed you why this works2 min read▶

"Drawing someone into motion" has a plain mechanical description: you are moving their centre of mass toward the edge of their base of support. Once it is near the edge, the force needed to take it past the edge is small, because the thing doing most of the work is their own weight.

The lab already showed you how little travel that takes. In the parallel stance the margin was about 12.5 cm; on one leg it was about 4.5 cm, with roughly 2.6 degrees of recoverable lean. Those are not large numbers. Get someone's centre out to that boundary and a very small nudge finishes it - four taels, near enough.

This is one of the places where a classical claim and ordinary mechanics say the same thing, and it is worth noticing that the honest version of the claim is the one that survives the check. The mangled version ("a tiny force beats a huge force") does not. The full version ("draw them into motion first, then a tiny force is enough") does.

Reflect & apply · 🧩 kit step 4

Your style shortlist ✦ 10 min · builds your kit

You now know enough to have preferences, which means the kit can stop being descriptive and start being a plan. Step 4 turns today's material into two things: a note of what actually interested you, and the beginning of a list of real classes to look into. Session 6 supplies the vetting method; today just gathers candidates.

Note what pulled you. Of the eight gates and five steps, which one did you want to know more about? There is no correct answer and the answer is informative. Someone drawn to 掤 and 中定 wants a body-mechanics class. Someone drawn to 采, 挒 and 推手 wants a martially-inclined teacher, and should know in advance that those are rarer.

Start a shortlist of two or three. Local classes, community-centre programmes, styles you keep seeing named. Do not evaluate them yet - Session 6 gives you the checklist, including the uncomfortable fact that there is no national certification standard in most countries. Just write down what exists near you and what style each one claims.

Write the one-line push hands summary in your own words, and keep it. It is there so that a class which never does push hands does not disappoint you, and a class which does does not alarm you.

Homework

Before Session 5 ◐ 25-35 min total

  • Keep the journal and the kit going. The daily two-minute song audit from Session 3 continues. Add one line a day about where your centre was during any ordinary transition - getting out of a chair counts.
  • Watch a push-hands video from any source, and watch for one thing only: how little visible force there is. If what you are watching looks like wrestling, you have found a competitive tuishou clip rather than the collaborative kind, and the difference is worth seeing too.
  • The 牵动 test, and it is a good one. Find one English-language tai chi article that quotes "four ounces deflects a thousand pounds." Check two things: does it credit the line to 太极拳论 rather than 打手歌, and does it include the 牵动, the drawing into motion? Most do neither. You now read this literature better than most of the people writing it, which is a strange but genuine feeling.
  • Optional: read the 打手歌 in full at Brennan Translation. It is six lines. The famous one is the fourth, and the line immediately after it is 引进落空合即出 - lead them in, let them fall into emptiness, join and issue - which is the same mechanism stated again.

Check yourself

Three questions before you go 🎯 ◐ 90 seconds

1 · 中定 zhōng dìng, central equilibrium, is...

You do not do 中定 instead of advancing; you advance while maintaining it. The lab's margin figure - centre of mass inside the base of support - is 中定 with a number on it.

2 · The trigram and five-phase correspondences for the thirteen postures are...

Two live discrepancies. The trigram assignment shifts with pre-heaven versus post-heaven arrangements, and the five-phase mapping in the 1881 manuscript differs from the 清代杨氏传抄老谱 version that almost everyone teaches. The eight-gate and five-step structure underneath is stable and old.

3 · 四两拨千斤 "four taels deflect a thousand catties" comes from...

The Treatise quotes it as an existing saying: 察四两拨千斤之句 - "examining the line." 句 jù, "the line," is decisive. And 牵动 is the mechanism, not decoration: without first pulling the opponent off balance, four taels deflect nothing.
